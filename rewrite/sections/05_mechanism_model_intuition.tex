\section{Where the Losses Fall}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\subsection{Interest rates and predetermined equity exposure}

The carry result contains a cross-sectional ordering before any beta is estimated. The portfolio loses because the high-interest-rate currencies it buys depreciate relative to the low-interest-rate currencies used for funding. The bilateral regression
\[
    \Delta s_{i,t+1}=a_i+b_iS_t+e_{i,t+1}
\]
asks whether a second predetermined characteristic orders the nine currency coefficients.

Australia and New Zealand have average S\&P equity betas of 0.20 and 0.13 and annualized state spot coefficients of $-13.21$ and $-13.28$ percentage points. Japan and Switzerland have betas near zero and coefficients of $+2.35$ and $-0.89$. Regressing the nine state coefficients on average predetermined beta produces a slope near $-60$ percentage points per beta unit, an intercept of $-0.75$, and $R^2=0.77$.

The nine-point gradient is economically sharp and statistically limited. It has no displayed confidence region for the fitted intercept, and Australia and New Zealand exert substantial leverage. It does not establish a structural beta or prove that exposure vanishes at zero. It documents a descriptive restriction that a uniform dollar shift does not match: in the S\&P-based ranking, currencies with greater predetermined market exposure have larger state-conditioned losses.

The beta-sorted portfolio supplies a portfolio-level implementation of that restriction. It buys the three currencies with the highest expanding-window betas and sells the three lowest, using estimates available before the return. Its spot return is 7.53 percentage points per year lower in the state, with episode-bootstrap $p=0.010$. The monthly panel interaction between the state and predetermined beta is also negative, about $-27$ points per beta unit, but its episode-bootstrap $p$-value is 0.210. The portfolio and bilateral gradient carry the exposure evidence; the panel interaction shows that its precision should not be inferred from the number of currency-month observations.

\subsection{The cluster and each currency's own curve}

Country-level regressions compare four objects: a currency's own live state alone, the synchronized cluster alone, both together, and a full specification adding own-slope variables and the average G10 slope. The cluster in these regressions still includes the predicted country's curve. This is an own-curve horse race, not a leave-one-country-out design.

The Australian own state is the only own-state coefficient detected on its own: $-8.76$ points with $p=0.044$. Conditional on the cluster, every own-state coefficient is imprecise. Cluster-alone estimates are $-13.21$ for Australia ($p=0.015$) and $-13.28$ for New Zealand ($p=0.011$). In the full specification their point estimates remain negative, at $-12.92$ and $-8.56$, but episode-bootstrap precision weakens. Across the pooled bilateral panel, the cluster coefficient is $-4.74$ with Newey--West $t=-2.34$, while the pooled own-state coefficient is $-0.01$ with $t=-0.01$.

Own-curve controls are jointly detected for Japan and not for the other eight currencies. This is an economically useful exception: the bundle of Japanese own-state, relative-slope, and relative-level controls is jointly detected even though the yen's cluster coefficient is positive. The joint test does not isolate which own-curve component supplies the information. More generally, the own-curve specifications do not reproduce the adverse long-minus-short portfolio pattern. A stronger claim—that other countries' curves predict a currency after its own curve is excluded—requires the unavailable country-month 10Y--2Y state matrix.

\subsection{Persistent portfolio cores with some rotation}

The rate and beta sorts hold persistent currency blocs. New Zealand occupies the carry long leg in 85 percent of all months and 91 percent of active-state months; Australia does so in 71 and 73 percent. Japan is in the funding leg in 83 percent of all months and 97 percent of active months; Switzerland's corresponding shares are 96 and 100 percent. For the beta sort, Canada is always in the long leg, Australia is long in 97 percent of all months and 92 percent of state months, and New Zealand in 77 and 76 percent.

The state-conditioned portfolios retain persistent cores, so the result is not created solely by resorting when the signal turns on. There is meaningful rotation around those cores: Norway's carry-long share falls from 62 percent overall to 34 percent in state months, while the pound's rises from 43 to 70 percent. Interest rates matter twice: they deliver known carry income and identify a persistent long-leg core that bears the spot loss. The sample rank correlation between average rate and beta rankings is 0.72. Country size, commodity exposure, trade position, and intermediary demand can all contribute to that alignment \citep{hassan2013,ready2017,dellaCorte2016,richmond2019,hassanMano2019,gabaixMaggiori2015}. The analysis does not select a unique primitive; it shows that two predetermined rankings point toward the same loss-bearing currencies.

\subsection{How stable is the beta ordering?}

Across the reported state-definition neighborhood, the S\&P-beta portfolio is more stable than carry. Its spot coefficient is $-10.35$ under one-month release, $-4.23$ under three-month release, and $-5.25$ under the 10Y--3M construction; all three are statistically detected at the reported levels. Removing fitted exposure to the U.S. ACM slope term premium leaves a $-6.17$-point beta coefficient with $p=0.004$, even as the carry coefficient attenuates.

The exposure measure itself has an important failure. Rebuilding the portfolio with expanding MSCI World betas produces a full-sample spot coefficient of $-2.93$ with $p=0.319$, compared with $-7.93$ and $p=0.005$ for S\&P betas on the same maximal sample. After 1999, both definitions are negative and detected. Early Japanese equity weight can explain why an external U.S. index and a capitalization-weighted world index rank the yen differently, but it does not erase the full-sample benchmark dependence.
