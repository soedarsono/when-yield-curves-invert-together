\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Currency portfolios diversify national shocks but remain exposed when several currencies move together. This distinction is central to the carry trade. Carry buys a standing set of high-interest-rate currencies and funds it with low-interest-rate currencies. The interest differential accrues gradually; one shared depreciation of the long leg can erase many months of income. This paper asks whether the cross-country configuration of bond markets reveals that loss state before the currency payoff.

One yield-curve inversion is ambiguous. It can reflect domestic weakness, country-specific disinflation, or a national term-premium movement. A cluster of fresh inversions is a different object: several bond markets cross the same economically salient boundary within overlapping windows. I construct a monthly state from 10-year/2-year slopes in nine non-dollar G10 economies. A fresh inversion observed in month $t-1$ becomes live in month $t$ and remains live until its slope has risen for two consecutive months. The state is active when at least two curves are live. The month-$t$ state predicts the return in month $t+1$.

The first result is a large conditional spot-return shift. The 1988--2026 sample contains 92 active months in fifteen episodes. Annualized carry spot returns average 2.85 percent outside the state and $-10.00$ percent inside it, a difference of $-12.85$ percentage points. The money-market-differential total-return proxy falls from 6.45 to $-4.86$ percent. The known interest differential remains positive, but it absorbs only part of the depreciation. The location shift reaches beyond the worst observations: the monthly spot median falls from 0.35 to $-0.76$ percent, and an outcome-conditioned bottom-decile deletion leaves a $-7.24$-point spot difference.

The second result isolates the cross-country configuration. Separate indicator regressions give a positive coefficient for exactly one live inversion and a $-15.72$-point annualized coefficient for exactly two; they do not provide a direct one-versus-two contrast. In joint regressions, the cluster retains predictive content alongside the U.S. inversion, the continuous average G10 slope and its change, and all nine country-level live states. The raw count and nearby release and tenor rules reveal meaningful sensitivity, not a universal cutoff: the 10Y--3M and three-month-release constructions attenuate the carry coefficient. The empirical object is therefore the nonlinear configuration of fresh crossings, with a precise historical estimate near the baseline rule, rather than “inversion” in general.

The third result shows where the loss lands. High-interest-rate currencies depreciate relative to the low-rate currencies used to fund carry. Bilateral state coefficients also become more negative with currencies' predetermined equity-market betas. The cross-currency gradient explains 77 percent of the variation across nine coefficients; a portfolio sorted directly on expanding-window S\&P 500 betas loses 7.53 percentage points per year in spot returns in the state. The exposure result is not uniform across every benchmark: an MSCI World beta sort is weak over the maximal full sample but agrees after 1999. This pattern makes the cross-sectional content sharper than a generic dollar or volatility state while keeping the beta measure itself contestable.

The paper's contribution is to change the unit of observation. National yield slopes forecast activity and combine expected short-rate paths with term premia \citep{harvey1988,estrellaHardouvelis1991,estrellaMishkin1998,campbellShiller1991,hamiltonKim2002}. International curves share important variation \citep{plosserRouwenhorst1994,bernardGerlach1998,diebold2008,chinnKucko2015}. Carry returns, in turn, load on common currency and stress factors \citep{fama1984,brunnermeier2008,lustig2011,menkhoff2012,farhiGabaix2016}. The new empirical object is neither one country's slope nor a smooth global average. It is a cross-country threshold configuration, dated before the payoff and paired with a predetermined exposure gradient.

That configuration produces a disciplined economic interpretation. Write national slopes as a shared component plus country-specific information. Several fresh threshold crossings are more consistent with shared information than one crossing under restrictions on the idiosyncratic components. If currencies differ in their exposure to a subsequent adverse return component, the losses should be ordered across currencies. The exact-one/exact-two contrast, the reported curve-state horse race, and the beta gradient implement those predictions. They support a shared-information interpretation; they do not distinguish global growth, coordinated policy, inflation, term premia, or intermediary risk-bearing as the primitive shock.

Two design facts govern the strength of the claim. First, fifteen episodes supply the effective common-state variation. Second, the tenor, threshold, freshness rule, and release rule were assessed on the same historical sample. I therefore show the reported local state-definition neighborhood and add an independently executable public 10Y--3M audit that exhaustively reports a declared 64-rule family. The public family frequently yields negative coefficients, but no rule meets the 5-percent common-calendar rotation-reference criterion. That adverse result places the ambition of the paper where the evidence is strongest: a novel cross-country configuration and exposure pattern, not an immaculate universal trading rule.

A public state that forecasts a negative total-return proxy also raises a pricing question. Predetermined carry compensates exposure only if it adjusts enough before the loss. A compact benchmark shows the restriction required for current expected losses to exceed both ordinary income and previously embedded compensation. It does not identify slow adjustment. The empirical contribution is prediction and exposure ordering; the friction remains an open mechanism.
